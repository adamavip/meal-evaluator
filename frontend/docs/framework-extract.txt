## A New MEAL Approach for CIMMYT
Better evidence. Better decisions. Greater impact.
[TABLE]
Why now? The operating environment for agricultural research has changed. Major investors, including bilateral agencies, foundations and governments, now ask for evidence of impact as well as delivery outputs. NGOs, national research systems, universities and private companies are all investing in better evidence systems, and CIMMYT competes with them for the same funding. Institutions that can demonstrate what changed, how, for whom, and why will be better positioned to attract funding, influence policy and sustain partnerships. Those that cannot will increasingly be asked to justify their relevance. This is why CIMMYT’s MEAL function needs to change.
[/TABLE]
CIMMYT is changing how it monitors, evaluates and learns. Instead of compiling what projects report, it will build a traceable body of evidence on what changed, for whom, where, under what conditions and at what scale. This requires new methods and a new way for projects, MEAL, data teams and scientists to work together.
CIMMYT already produces a large amount of evidence through trials, producer registries, field surveys, research platforms, administrative systems, sensors, satellite imagery, digital tools and partner systems. The task is to connect these sources through common questions, indicators, identifiers and quality rules, so that evidence can be reused across projects and aggregated at portfolio and institutional level.
When monitoring is built mainly from project reports and aggregated figures, CIMMYT can describe delivery but cannot consistently reconstruct what changed on the ground. A report can confirm that training took place or that farmers were reached; it cannot, by itself, show whether practices changed, yields improved, risks declined,  benefits persisted or any changes in the livelihoods. The new approach therefore works from the underlying evidence: observation-level records from farmers, plots, trials, households and institutions, combined with geospatial and contextual data when these strengthen the measurement.
[TABLE]
Reporting summaries stay. The objective is to connect them to observation-level data that can be analyzed, aggregated and used for learning and impact.
[/TABLE]

## 1.3  MEAL in the Context of CIMMYT
CIMMYT is a global agricultural research institution with 50+ years of maize and wheat improvement work, a genebank conserving over 180,000 seed accessions, trial networks spanning more than 90 countries, and partnerships with national agricultural research systems (NARS), extension services, seed companies, governments and farmers across the Global South.
This shapes what MEAL means at CIMMYT. Most of the evidence CIMMYT produces concerns scientific contribution to change in agrifood systems, more than service delivery. Its data (multi-environment trial results, adoption surveys, genomic data, remote sensing, agronomic datasets and socioeconomic studies) are a valuable institutional asset that is still underused for cross-portfolio learning and impact demonstration.
The framework connects breeding data from CIMMYT scientists, adoption evidence from socioeconomics teams, agronomic performance data from field programs and outcome data from development projects. Together they form one evidence base that covers CIMMYT’s full contribution to change.

## The MEAL Framework
From data to impact, across the results pyramid.
The framework organizes evidence across four levels of the results chain (Processes, Outputs, Outcomes and Impact) and five operational functions (Capture, Quality Assurance, Integration, Analysis, and Use & Visualization). The logic is cumulative, but responsibilities differ by level. Project teams generate and manage routine processes and output evidence. The institutional MEAL function sets standards and integrates evidence from outcomes upward, and works with projects early enough that the required data are generated correctly from the start.
CIMMYT generates and uses data at every step of the results chain to understand what works, for whom, in what context and at what scale. Different types of data serve different analytical purposes, and the framework does not ask all of them to carry the same evidential weight.
## 2.1  Processes
Processes are the activities, partnerships, and management actions that enable delivery. They represent the day-to-day execution of projects: workplan implementation, resource mobilization, coordination, financial tracking, partner engagement and risk management.
Data at this level come from activity tracking systems, workplans, financial records and partnership registries. The priority is completeness, accuracy and timeliness. Integration connects planning, finance and implementation data. Analysis focuses on process efficiency, implementation quality, bottlenecks and resource use. Products include activity dashboards, workplan trackers and resource-use monitoring.
[TABLE]
Process data has immediate management value: it allows teams to identify and address execution problems before they become outcome problems.
[/TABLE]
## 2.2  Outputs
Outputs are the products, services, technologies and capacity delivered through CIMMYT projects and partnerships. They include varieties developed, digital tools deployed, training delivered, recommendations produced, datasets published, platforms established and partnerships formalized.
Data come from delivery records, uptake registries, trial results, training logs and distribution systems. Quality work verifies completeness, removes duplicates and standardizes formats. Integration connects delivery data with baselines, targets and geospatial information. Analysis examines reach, coverage, quality, efficiency and initial uptake. Products include output dashboards, delivery trackers and adoption maps.
Counting what was delivered is the starting point. The purpose is to show where, how, to whom, with what quality and at what scale, which is the foundation for outcome-level evidence.
## 2.3  Outcomes
Outcomes reflect changes in behaviors, systems, practice, capacity, policy or performance among beneficiaries and partners. They are the level at which CIMMYT's contribution to change becomes most visible and most important to establish credibly.
Examples include adoption of climate-smart technologies; changes in agronomic practices; improved institutional capacity; policy implementation; stronger service delivery systems; and shifts in investment decisions.
Data come from adoption surveys, outcome studies, administrative records, partner performance data and digital monitoring. Quality work addresses consistency, missing values, outliers and comparability across projects. Integration combines information across projects, regions and time periods. Analysis focuses on contribution, outcome mapping, trends, heterogeneity by gender, age and territory, and factors associated with change. Products include outcome dashboards, beneficiary profiles, thematic maps and stories of change grounded in data.
[TABLE]
Outcomes answer the essential institutional question: are people, organizations and systems changing as a result of CIMMYT's interventions?
[/TABLE]
## 2.4  Impact
Impact is positive, lasting change in agrifood systems: productivity, incomes, food security, resilience, resource-use efficiency, emissions, biodiversity, inclusion and institutional transformation.
Data come from surveys, impact evaluations, longitudinal panels, remote sensing, external databases and models. Integration links impact data with outcomes, interventions, contextual data, climate information and market dynamics. Analysis can include counterfactual impact evaluations, attribution and contribution analysis, geospatial impact assessment, modeling and scenario analysis. Products include impact dashboards, geospatial maps, stories of change, contribution analyses and evaluation findings.
CIMMYT does not claim to be the sole cause of observed changes. The aim is credible evidence of contribution: the role that CIMMYT’s technologies, partnerships, capacity and services played, alongside other factors, in producing lasting change.
## 2.5  The Five Evidence Functions
Five functions run across the results pyramid. They provide a practical sequence for designing and operating MEAL in every project and for integrating evidence institution-wide:
[TABLE]
Capture | Identify the question first, then capture the observation-level and contextual data needed to answer it. Use direct field measurement, digital records, partner systems, remote sensing or secondary sources according to the indicator method.
Quality Assurance | Validate, standardize, de-duplicate and document provenance and quality. Controls cover completeness, accuracy, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness and representativeness.
Integration | Link records across sources, projects, partners, locations and time using common identifiers, controlled vocabularies and interoperable structures. Recompute institutional indicators from the underlying eligible records rather than summing project reports.
Analysis | Apply methods that match the question and claim: descriptive, performance, contribution, counterfactual, geospatial, modeling or scenario analysis. Explicitly document uncertainty and limits.
Use & Visualization | Turn evidence into dashboards, maps, briefs, learning reviews and decision products for specific users. The purpose is action: adaptation, accountability, strategy and impact demonstration.
[/TABLE]
Data systems serve MEAL. With the Data Group, CIMMYT will make sure evidence moves from collection to quality assurance, integration, analysis and use without repeated manual reporting. The MEAL data system (Section 4) keeps the underlying records and their provenance, so institutional results can be recalculated, audited and reused as methods improve.
## 2.6  The Evidence Stack
The MEAL data system does not treat a project report as the primary unit of evidence. It works as close as possible to what is being measured and combines complementary sources, so that each result can be checked from more than one angle. Not every indicator needs every source; the KPI reference sheet sets what is valid.
[TABLE]
Evidence layer | Typical sources | What it adds | Example use
1. Direct observations | Plot, farm, household, participant, trial, institution or result-object records | The closest evidence of the actual state or change; permits disaggregation and re-analysis | Crop cuts and plot records to estimate yield; farmer records to measure adoption
2. Earth observation & sensors | Satellite imagery, weather, sensors, geospatial layers | Spatial coverage, frequency and extrapolation between observed locations | Map land under practices after calibration with ground observations
3. External/context data | Government statistics, FAO and other public datasets, prices, climate and market series | Context, denominators, benchmarking and covariates needed to interpret change | Compare observed productivity changes with rainfall, prices or national trends
4. Operational records | Workplans, training logs, distribution systems, financial and partner records | Traceability from intervention to output and links between delivery and later outcomes | Link who received a service to later uptake or performance
[/TABLE]
Triangulation rule. A source strengthens a claim only when it measures the same construct, population, geography and period under compatible definitions. Satellite or secondary data do not replace ground observations where the KPI method requires direct measurement; they are used for calibration, contextualization, stratification or upscaling under documented rules.
## 2.7  Accountability in Practice
Accountability is the dimension of MEAL that is most often stated and least often operationalized. For CIMMYT, it is more than submitting reports to investors on time. It runs in four directions, each with different requirements:
[TABLE]
Accountability to | What it means in practice | How MEAL supports it
Investors & institutional partners | Transparent, timely and comparable reporting on what was delivered, what changed and what was learned, using evidence that meets the methodological standards investors now expect. | Harmonized KPIs, documented methodologies, governance over data quality, and synthesis across the portfolio into credible institutional evidence products.
Farmers, communities and relevant stakeholders | Showing that CIMMYT’s work produces real benefits for the smallholder farmers, households and communities it serves, and saying so openly when it does not. This includes disaggregating evidence by gender, age and other equity dimensions. | Outcome and impact measurement that is farmer-centered; disaggregation by gender and vulnerability; feedback mechanisms that return evidence to the communities it concerns.
The scientific community & CGIAR | Evidence that meets scientific standards of rigor, reproducibility and transparency and adds to the global knowledge base on agricultural transformation, beyond internal reporting. | FAIR data practices, documented methods, open publication of datasets and findings, and evaluation designs that support learning across the CGIAR system.
Internal | Individuals, leads and teams are accountable for the processes and outputs within their remit; leadership is accountable for portfolio-level results. | Easier access to synthesized information, e.g., unpacking breeding-portal data so that individual and team contributions are visible.
[/TABLE]
[TABLE]
Accountability means being open about what works, what does not, and what CIMMYT is learning: within CIMMYT, with investors, with the scientific community, and with the farmers and communities whose lives agricultural research is meant to improve.
[/TABLE]

## KPI Approach
One toolbox, methods fit for purpose, results that can be aggregated.
CIMMYT is building a KPI toolbox to guide monitoring across the results pyramid. Each KPI has defined methods for different levels of measurement and aggregation, so that evidence is comparable and answers the questions that matter.
KPI harmonization does not require every project to measure the same thing in the same way. It means that when projects measure the same concept, they share a definition, a methodological logic and rules that allow interpretation and, where valid, aggregation.
## 3.1  KPI Toolbox: Volumes and Thrusts
The KPI Toolbox holds 142 indicators organized in 13 volumes, each covering one pathway: ten impact pathways, which measure CIMMYT’s contribution to change, and three enabling pathways, which measure how CIMMYT itself performs. The impact-pathway volumes are grouped under CIMMYT’s three Thrusts, Human Wellbeing, Planetary Health and Peace, which are the top level at which institutional results are aggregated and reported. Each volume contains full reference sheets with definitions, methods, disaggregation, data sources and aggregation rules.
[TABLE]

2 | Capacity, data and institutions | Impact | 10 (5 / 5 / 0) | Peace
3 | Nutrition and food security | Impact | 12 (4 / 7 / 1) | Peace
4 | Genetic resources, breeding and seed systems | Impact | 11 (8 / 3 / 0) | Human Wellbeing
5 | Income and livelihoods | Impact | 9 (2 / 5 / 2) | Human Wellbeing
6 | Resilience and climate adaptation | Impact | 9 (2 / 3 / 4) | Planetary Health

Volume 1 applies across all three Thrusts, since innovations and partnerships feed every pathway. The three enabling volumes are reported alongside the Thrusts as CIMMYT’s institutional performance.
## 3.2  One KPI, Multiple Valid Measurement Levels
A common KPI does not imply one method everywhere. Yield, in tons per hectare, shows how one concept can be measured at different levels with different methods, each suited to the decision at hand:
[TABLE]
Level | How we measure it | Key standards | Quality rating
Farm / Plot (Parcel) | • Direct measurement • Crop cut • Farmer survey | • Use the most reliable method feasible in context • Document method used and assign quality rating | High to medium, depending on method
Trial / Station (On-farm / On-station) | • Direct measurement of harvested plot • Standard protocols | • Measure yield on net plot • Adjust to standard moisture (e.g. 12.5% for maize) • Minimum plot size / repetitions required | High (controlled protocols)
Region / Country (Aggregated) | • Aggregated from multiple trials and farm data • Modeling / spatial interpolation where needed | • Define inclusion criteria • Weight by area / representativeness • Report uncertainty bounds | Variable; document coverage and method quality
[/TABLE]
For every KPI, CIMMYT defines the concept, unit of observation, data sources, valid methods by measurement level, calculation and aggregation rules, disaggregation, quality assurance requirements, and data provenance. Projects may specify their local scope, but they do not silently redefine the institutional indicator. This is what makes evidence comparable and reusable across projects and contexts.
## 3.3  From Observation-Level Data to Decision-Relevant Insights
Data generated at different levels (plots, trials, farms, regions) have to be combined to produce comparable estimates at institutional scale.
Institutional values are recomputed from eligible underlying records using the method defined for the indicator. Depending on the KPI, this may involve de-duplication, weighting, pooling of microdata, extrapolation, spatial interpolation, modeling or meta-analysis, and triangulation with compatible secondary sources. The result is reported with its coverage, uncertainty, provenance and method quality. It is never an unqualified sum of project totals. Results are then rolled up by KPI volume and by Thrust.
[TABLE]
Example: What has CIMMYT contributed to yield change in Kenya? Answering this needs contribution or attribution analysis against a credible counterfactual, agronomic, socioeconomic and climate modeling, adoption data and geospatial assessment. A sum of reported numbers cannot answer it.
[/TABLE]
This approach builds five institutional capabilities:
▸  Common definitions and standards that enable comparability across projects, regions and years
▸  Foresight and better targeting: using past and current evidence to inform institutional strategy and future investment
▸  Quality and data assurance embedded throughout the evidence lifecycle
▸  Capacity and methodological guidance for teams at all levels
▸  Continuous improvement as the quality and use of evidence grow

## The MEAL Data Ecosystem
What the system must do so that evidence moves from collection to use without manual re-reporting.
The framework depends on a MEAL data ecosystem. At its center is a MEAL data system that holds observation-level records, applies common quality rules and calculates institutional indicators directly from those records. The system is built with the Data Group. It does not replace CIMMYT’s scientific and corporate systems or investors’ reporting systems: where another system is the authoritative source for an indicator (breeding, trials, finance, project management), the MEAL data system connects to it instead of copying data by hand.
The requirements below describe the functions the system needs, not a specific product. They can guide decisions on existing tools, new development or a combination of both.
## 4.1  Required Functionalities
The system must provide nine functionalities:
[TABLE]
Functionality | What the system must do | Why MEAL needs it
Multi-channel capture | Online/offline mobile collection, web forms and low-bandwidth channels (e.g., WhatsApp, IVR); import from partner systems, sensors and remote sensing; forms built from the KPI reference sheets. | Projects collect data once, in the format the KPI method requires.
Persistent identifiers | Unique, stable IDs for farmers, plots, households, organizations, trials and projects across projects and years. | Links delivery to later uptake and outcomes; prevents double counting.
Quality assurance & provenance | Validation at entry; automated completeness, range, consistency and duplicate checks; quality flags; audit trail and record-level provenance. | Institutional figures can be audited and recalculated.
Privacy, consent & access | Consent records, role-based access, protection of personal data and data-sharing rules with partners. | Compliance with CIMMYT data policy and investor requirements.
Integration | APIs to CIMMYT systems (breeding, trials, finance, project management) and external sources (climate, prices, national statistics); shared data dictionary and controlled vocabularies. | One evidence base across projects and sources.
KPI engine | Calculates institutional KPIs from eligible records with the approved method and reports coverage, uncertainty and method version. | Replaces summing project reports.
Analytics & geospatial | Statistical, geospatial and modeling workflows on integrated data, with reproducible scripts. | Outcome, contribution and impact analysis.
Dashboards & reporting | Role-based dashboards (project, regional, institutional), maps, standard reporting packs, and exports in investor and CGIAR formats. | Evidence reaches decision-makers on time.
Learning & feedback | Decision logs, management responses and participant feedback linked to the evidence behind them. | Closes the loop from evidence to action.
[/TABLE]
## 4.2  Design Principles
The system follows open standards and FAIR principles. It works offline where connectivity is poor, and it is modular, so each project configures what it needs from shared components. Responsibility is split: the Data Group owns the infrastructure, and the MEAL team owns the content (indicators, data dictionary and quality rules).
## 4.3  Alignment with Investor Reporting Systems: Collect Data Once
CIMMYT has to feed many reporting systems: CGIAR’s, such as the Performance and Results Management System (PRMS), investors’ own platforms and templates, and in some cases national or partner systems. The MEAL data system is built to adapt to any of them. For each system, the MEAL team maps the required fields to the source variables already collected and sets up the connection: a direct, automated link where the receiving system allows it, and an export in the required format where it does not. Every submission is generated from the same validated records used for institutional KPIs, so projects collect data once and do not enter the same result again for each system.
Adding a new reporting system, or adjusting to changed requirements, means adding or updating a mapping, not creating a new data collection. The MEAL team does this centrally, and projects keep collecting through the same forms.

## MEAL Operating Model & Roadmap
How MEAL is designed into projects, handed over for implementation, and integrated into institutional evidence.
The framework is implemented through two connected cycles: a project lifecycle, which makes sure MEAL is designed and resourced before implementation starts, and an institutional rollout, which gradually embeds the KPI toolbox, systems and capacities across the portfolio. The central MEAL team does not replace project-level monitoring. Its role is to set standards, enable teams, assure quality and integrate evidence so that outcomes and impact can be understood across projects.

## 5.2  Division of Responsibility: Project Delivery vs. Institutional MEAL
The operating principle is “central standards, distributed implementation, institutional integration.” Project teams remain accountable for generating the evidence closest to delivery; the central MEAL team ensures that the evidence can support comparable outcome and impact analysis across CIMMYT.

## 5.3  Governance
Three rules keep the system coherent as it grows.
1. KPI Toolbox: No new institutional KPI is added, and no existing KPI is redefined, without review by the MEAL team. Projects can add project-specific indicators, but these are not reported as institutional KPIs until the Design, Standards & Enablement pillar has reviewed and approved them. Approved changes are published in a new version of the MEAL Handbook.
2. Proposal review: Every proposal goes through the MEAL team at proposal stage, where the MEAL Matrix sets its scale and M&E complexity (Annex A). Small projects can proceed directly with the standard MEAL package, provided they include the base MEAL budget for their complexity level (USD 8,000 to 18,000). Medium projects confirm their MEAL budget with the team while the proposal is written. Large and MEGA projects co-design MEAL with the team from the start of proposal development.
3. Decisions and exceptions: The MEAL Group Lead is accountable for implementing changes to the Handbook and for exceptions to KPI methods. Once a year, a MEAL Review Committee reviews the Handbook, the KPI Toolbox and the MEAL Matrix, using the results of the annual KPI run and the evidence-gap register, and endorses the changes for the following year. The Committee is an institutional body, not an internal MEAL one: it sits at the level of CIMMYT’s scientific leadership, and the MEAL Group Lead prepares and presents the annual review to it.
## 5.4  Minimum MEAL Implementation Package
Every new project should leave inception with the following minimum package. The depth varies by scale, but none of these elements should be left to end-of-project reporting.
1. Results architecture: Theory of Change / results chain, decision and learning questions, and links to CIMMYT Strategy.
2. Indicator crosswalk: Applicable institutional KPIs, investor indicators and project-specific measures, with no silent redefinition of institutional indicators.
3. Measurement plan: Unit of observation, data source, valid method, frequency, sample/coverage, disaggregation, responsible owner and timing for each indicator.
4. Data architecture: Authoritative systems, persistent identifiers, integration points, access rules, consent/privacy requirements and planned data flows.
5. Quality plan: Validation rules, first-line checks, deduplication, exception handling, provenance, audit trail and periodic MEAL assurance.
6. Baseline / reference: Baseline, comparison group or other valid reference where the outcome/impact method requires it; otherwise a documented alternative.
7. Learning & accountability cycle: Calendar for evidence reviews, adaptation decisions, participant/partner feedback and communication back to relevant stakeholders.
8. Handover & capacity: Named project MEAL/data owners, training completed, reusable guidance available and escalation route to central MEAL/Data Group.

## 5.8  MEAL Team Structure and Roles
The MEAL function is organized in three connected layers. The Core Layer, made up of the MEAL Group Lead and three technical pillars, provides institutional standards, specialist expertise, systems and portfolio-level analysis. The Regional Layer makes these approaches work across each regional portfolio. The Project Layer, the Extended MEAL Network of Project MEAL Focal Points, keeps monitoring and evidence generation close to implementation. The structure is deliberately lean: thirteen positions in the Core and Regional Layers, with focal points added as the portfolio grows.

## 5.9  How the Roles Work Together
Each task has one owner, and handoffs between pillars are explicit. The Design pillar defines what must be measured and the valid method; Evidence Systems specifies how the evidence is structured, quality-assured, aggregated and visualized; Evaluation, Learning & Accountability addresses questions that routine monitoring cannot answer and closes the evidence-to-action loop. Regional MEAL Leads bring these capabilities to each regional portfolio, while Project MEAL Focal Points remain responsible for day-to-day project monitoring and source evidence. Advanced geospatial, software-development, data-science and corporate data-governance capabilities are mobilized through CIMMYT's relevant technical functions rather than duplicated inside MEAL.
## 5.10  Funding the MEAL Function
The MEAL function is funded from three sources, and the balance between them is expected to shift over time.
1. Base contribution from every project: Each project budgets a base MEAL contribution set in the MEAL Matrix according to its scale and M&E complexity (table below and Annex A). It pays for the institutional services every project receives: standards and guidance, proposal review, quality assurance, integration into the MEAL data system, portfolio reporting and PRMS and investor reporting. Large and MEGA projects draw most on the team’s capacity, so they are expected to fund a significant share of the Core Layer through charged staff time.

2. 3. New resources: The team co-develops proposals in which evaluation, learning or data systems are a substantial component, and seeks funding for cross-portfolio evidence work such as impact studies and syntheses.
Most projects active in 2026 and 2027 were designed before the base contribution existed, so income from it will grow gradually as new projects start. Core and Regional Layer positions will need bridge funding until enough projects contribute to cover them. The MEAL Matrix amounts will be reviewed every year against the actual cost of the team, the number of active projects and their complexity mix.
